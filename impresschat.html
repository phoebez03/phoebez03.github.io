ImpressChat

A mobile learning app that teaches Impressionist art through chat-based interaction — guiding learners through structured conversations with historical figures, POE activities, and immediate AI-graded feedback. Accepted to ACM Learning@Scale 2026 in Seoul.

Overview

Art education online is mostly passive.
ImpressChat is not.

Most online art courses ask learners to read articles and watch videos. They deliver content, but they don't build the perceptual skills that art understanding actually requires — the ability to look closely, reason about what you see, and connect visual details to historical context. That kind of skill develops through practice, not consumption.

ImpressChat addresses this by putting interaction first. Learners don't scroll through facts about Monet — they chat with him. They're prompted to make predictions before receiving any instruction, then observe visual evidence, then explain their reasoning. The app measures learning through parallel pre- and post-tests so growth is visible.

The Problem

Online art education prioritizes content delivery over active analysis, making it hard for learners to develop perceptual and interpretive skills that only come from genuine engagement with visual material.

The Audience

Adult learners approaching Impressionism for the first time — whether in a classroom, a museum workshop, or self-directed. The app needed to work without an instructor present.

My Contribution

I designed the full interface in Figma — every screen, interaction state, and feedback display — while also contributing to the instructional design of the learning modules and the course structure.

The Outcome

ImpressChat was accepted as a demo and poster presentation at ACM Learning@Scale 2026 in Seoul, one of the leading international conferences on technology-supported learning at scale.

Design Process

Backwards design.
Two rounds of testing.

ImpressChat followed a backwards design process — we defined learning objectives first, then mapped the user flow, then built from low-fidelity wireframes to high-fidelity Figma prototypes before the developer implemented in Xcode. Design decisions were always traced back to a specific learning goal.

Phase 1

Learning objectives

Define LOs, map pre/post-test structure and module flow

Phase 2

Lo-fi wireframes

Sketch chat interaction patterns, navigation, feedback layouts

Phase 3

Think-aloud testing

Figma prototype testing revealed cognitive overload in message density

Phase 4

Implemented app testing

Live Xcode build surfaced terminology confusion and technical bugs

Phase 5

Final build

Refined flows, confirmed assessment structure, submitted to L@S

Phases 3 and 4 — the two testing rounds — drove the most significant design changes.

01
Design Decision

Breaking messages apart
to reduce cognitive load

In the first round of think-aloud testing on Figma prototypes, users were visibly overwhelmed by the chat interface. Long blocks of instructional text landed in a single message bubble — and even though the content was well-designed, learners stopped reading before they reached the key point. The medium was working against the message.

The fix was structural: I broke instructional content into smaller, sequentially revealed message chunks, and tuned the timing between them. This wasn't just aesthetic — it matched how people actually read in a chat context: one idea at a time, with space to process before the next arrives. Shorter messages also made the response options easier to locate, reducing the scanning work required at every interaction step.

Chunked message delivery

Instructional content split into 1–2 sentence bursts, each visually distinct, so learners absorb one idea before the next appears. Reduced the amount of text visible at any one time.

Timed reveal sequence

Messages appeared with deliberate pacing rather than all at once, mimicking a real conversation rhythm and giving learners a moment to engage before more content arrived.

Persistent response options

Answer choices anchored at the bottom of the screen at all times, so users never had to scroll to find them — a friction point the first prototype hadn't accounted for.

Visual keyword highlighting

Key art terms (e.g., "light," "atmosphere," "brushwork") visually highlighted in feedback messages to anchor understanding and aid recall of terminology.

02
Design Decision

Fixing terminology and
navigation on the live build

The second round of testing happened on the implemented Xcode build, and it surfaced a different category of problems: terminology confusion and bugs that only appeared under real interaction conditions. Learners weren't sure what "Module" meant in context, couldn't tell whether they'd completed a section, and in some cases got stuck when the app didn't recover gracefully from an unexpected input.

This round was valuable precisely because it was messier than Figma testing. Real implementation reveals friction that even high-fidelity prototypes can paper over — the exact moment a transition feels abrupt, the specific label that makes someone pause. I iterated directly from session notes, prioritising fixes that affected comprehension over fixes that affected polish.

Changed

Module labels rewritten in plain language — from system-centric labels ("Module 2") to content-centric ones ("Chat with Monet") so learners understood what was coming, not just where they were in a sequence.

Changed

Completion states added to module cards on the course outline screen, giving learners a clear visual record of progress and reducing re-entry confusion when returning to the app.

Confirmed

The core chat interaction model held up under real use. Learners understood the predict-before-instruction structure without explanation, validating the scaffolded response-option design.

Confirmed

The pretest-to-posttest arc felt meaningful to learners. Seeing their score improve gave the app a clear sense of purpose — learning was visible, not just felt.

Learning Design

Three principles.
One coherent learning arc.

My role wasn't only the interface — I also contributed to the instructional design of the modules. Every design decision in the UI maps back to a specific learning science principle. The visual design isn't decoration on top of the pedagogy; it is the pedagogy made visible.

Structured chat to scaffold interaction

Conversational interfaces activate social responses and promote active cognitive processing. Fixed response options scaffold learners without removing agency, and manage cognitive load by constraining the decision space.

Predict–Observe–Explain

Learners predict before any instruction is delivered. This activates prior knowledge and creates a cognitive need for the content that follows. POE activities consistently produce deeper understanding than passive reading.

Immediate, targeted feedback

Corrective and explanatory feedback is embedded directly into the interaction — not shown on a results screen at the end. Feedback names the concept, explains the reasoning, and highlights the visual evidence in the painting.

The three learning objectives drove every assessment and module decision:

Learning Objective Pre/Post-test Questions Instructional Module
Recall — sequence and contextualise Impressionist movements Q1 MCQ   Q2 Open Response Module 1 — What is Impressionism? Ask the Art Historian
Classify — identify Impressionist works from a mixed set Q3 Image MCQ Module 2 — The Artists: Chat with Monet & Degas
Evaluate — attribute unseen paintings to specific artists Q4 Image MCQ   Q5 Dropdown cloze Module 3 — Post-Impressionism: Chat with Roger Fry

The pre- and post-tests are structurally parallel — same question types, same learning objectives, different paintings. Growth is real, not just familiar.

This parallel structure was a deliberate instructional design choice. If the post-test used the same artworks as the pre-test, an improvement score could reflect recognition rather than understanding. By using new, unseen paintings that test the same skills, the assessment actually measures whether learners can transfer what they've learned — the bar that makes educational measurement meaningful.

Reflections

The chat interface only works as a learning tool if the design earns trust at every step — the learner has to believe the conversation is worth slowing down for.

— ImpressChat · Reflection after L@S submission · 2026

Building ImpressChat taught me that mobile learning design lives or dies at the sentence level. A block of instructional text that looks fine in a Figma frame becomes an obstacle in a real chat interface — and no amount of good pedagogy recovers a learner who has already stopped reading. The two testing rounds weren't checkboxes; they were the actual design work.

Do differently

Run a cognitive load check on every message before prototyping, not after. I caught the message density problem in the first think-aloud, but an earlier audit of word count per bubble would have saved a full iteration cycle.

Do differently

Test navigation labels earlier in the process. The terminology confusion in round two was a solvable problem — but it only surfaced on the live build because we'd assumed shared vocabulary between the team and the learner. That assumption was wrong.

Carry forward

Backwards design keeps the team honest. Starting from learning objectives meant every interface decision had a clear answer to "why does this exist?" It kept scope tight and gave us a shared language for saying no to ideas that felt good but didn't serve a learning goal.

Carry forward

The POE structure is transferable beyond art. Making learners commit to a prediction before any instruction is one of the highest-leverage moves in instructional design — it works because it gives learners a stake in the answer before they receive it. I'd use this in future projects across domains.
