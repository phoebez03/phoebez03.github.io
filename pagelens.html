PageLens

A concept prototype for an AI-powered AR reading companion that turns any physical library book into a dyslexia-friendly experience — proposed for the Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh's reading stations, and developed through user research, technology feasibility studies, and full hardware specification.

Overview

15–20% of readers have dyslexia.
Public libraries weren't built for them.

The Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh holds an extraordinarily rich print collection — novels, textbooks, newspapers, historical archives. But print is fixed: it can't adjust size, spacing, or layout to a reader's needs, and it offers nothing in the moment reading actually breaks down. For dyslexic readers, that gap is compounded by stigma — many hide their disability rather than risk appearing different.

Dyslexia isn't a matter of intelligence — it's a difference in how the brain processes written language. Readers commonly describe letters that seem to move, flip, or blur, and words that crowd together mid-line. Underneath that visual experience is a phonological processing difference: even a word that's clearly seen takes significantly more cognitive effort to decode into meaning.

The Problem

Print books can't adapt to their reader, and existing digital accessibility tools solve this only by asking readers to abandon the physical book entirely.

The Concept Space

We chose the Carnegie Library of Pittsburgh's Oakland branch — a historic public library whose mission of open access mirrors PageLens's own inclusive-design goals.

The Research Base

Every feature traces back to a specific citation — from eye-tracking studies on letter spacing to feasibility papers on generative-AI text simplification.

The Format

This is a concept prototype, not a shipped product. The deliverable is a process book, a technical feasibility study, and a device concept — not a working app.

Design Process

From a fixed idea
to a flexible one.

Current reading systems assume a one-size-fits-all format: text is fixed, no support is offered mid-read, and readers must adapt themselves to the material. The opportunity was to introduce adaptive support into a real-world reading context without abandoning the physical book — a system that supports spacing, simplification, audio, and visualization at the same time, shifting reading from fixed to personalized.

01
Design Decision

Rejecting the
"dyslexia font" myth

Our first instinct was to render every page in a specialized dyslexia typeface — it's the most visible, most marketed "solution" out there. The literature said otherwise. Wery & Diliberto (2017), published in Annals of Dyslexia, found that OpenDyslexic actually reduced reading rate and accuracy compared to standard fonts — no student in the study preferred it. What the evidence does support: increased letter spacing to reduce visual crowding, shortened line length (Schneps et al., 2013), and keyword highlighting as a visual anchor for tracking meaning (Rello et al., 2014).

That reversed our typography direction entirely. PageLens renders extracted text in a plain, familiar sans-serif — Arial by default — with adjustable spacing and highlighting, the version the research actually supports rather than the version that looks most like an "accessibility feature." OpenDyslexic isn't gone, though — readers can still switch to it in typography settings if they prefer it; it's just no longer the default the system leads with.

Standard sans-serif, not a specialty font

Arial as the default typeface — matching what readers actually preferred across studies, not what looks most like an assistive tool. OpenDyslexic remains available as a customization option for readers who prefer it, even though the evidence doesn't favor it.

Increased letter spacing

Separates characters that otherwise blur together, directly addressing the visual crowding readers describe most often.

Shortened line length

Reduces the chance of losing one's place mid-paragraph (Schneps et al., 2013).

Keyword highlighting

Gives readers a visual anchor for tracking meaning across a sentence (Rello et al., 2014).

02
Design Decision

Naming features
without naming a diagnosis

Stigma showed up in the research as a real, documented barrier — people may hide a disability specifically to avoid appearing different (Almgren Bäck et al., 2024; Dawson et al., 2019; ATIA, 2024). That shaped both the product's tone and its physical form. Every feature is named for what it does, not what it's for: Easy to Read, Simplify Words, Read Aloud, and Bring Story to Life — never "dyslexia mode" or "reading assistance."

The same logic drove the hardware decision to build PageLens as a familiar, mainstream-looking device rather than a visibly clinical tool. Support delivered through something that looks like it belongs on any library desk is less visible, and less stigmatizing, than a device that announces what it's for.

Principle

PageLens is built around the social model of disability: the barrier isn't the reader, it's the text. Every feature reduces friction without requiring the user to disclose or justify their needs.

Principle

Onboarding asks for an age band (Child, Teen, Adult), not a diagnosis — a single, low-stakes choice that quietly personalizes narration voice and simplification strength.

Acknowledged limit

This framing has a real edge: users with limited digital literacy, or with visual or motor impairments beyond the current feature set, may still find the experience insufficient.

Acknowledged limit

Age-based personalization only meaningfully affects two features — simplification and narration voice. Story illustrations follow the book's own visual world instead.

Technology & Feasibility

Three technologies, evaluated
on what they're actually good at.

Augmented reality was selected for its ability to overlay digital aids directly onto physical text, reducing the cognitive load of switching attention between a reference source and the page itself (Marner et al., 2013). Generative AI (GPT‑4/LLM) transforms complex text into simplified language and visuals in real time, and OCR extracts text from the physical page — reaching up to 99.99% accuracy for common printed fonts under good lighting (Kuerban et al., 2025).

Each technology also has real limits we designed around: handheld tablets cause significant fatigue over time, especially for children (Marner et al., 2013; Ridzwan et al., 2025); OCR accuracy drops in poor lighting; and dyslexic readers typically have shorter attention spans, making sessions past 15–20 minutes less effective (Ridzwan et al., 2025). Those constraints are why PageLens isn't a handheld tablet — it's a stabilized station.

Easy to Read

Wider letter spacing and shorter lines, adjustable in real time — the typography changes with real evidence behind them (Wery & Diliberto, 2017). Arial is the default in place of specialty dyslexia fonts, though OpenDyslexic remains available for readers who prefer it.

Simplify Words

A short tap swaps a difficult word for a simpler one in place, powered by GPT‑4/LLM text simplification (Kuerban et al., 2025).

Read Aloud

Multisensory teaching research (Birsh, 2011; Berninger et al., 2015) supports pairing visual text with audio; a long press reads a sentence aloud through headphones (Wood et al., 2017).

Bring Story to Life

AR illustrations appear at key story moments, turning abstract concepts into visuals for readers who think spatially (Alexander et al., 1991).

From Handheld to Stationed

Three feasibility problems reshaped the physical device over the course of the project:

Page turning

Music-stand-style clamps were taxing to turn pages against. The fix: custom spring clamps calibrated to the minimum hold force needed to counter page curl, with flared edges to guide pages underneath.

Spine protection

A flat cradle couldn't serve every binding. The fix: a motorized V-hinge that sets angle by book type on identification — roughly 180° for hardcovers, 150–160° for paperbacks to prevent glue cracking.

Portability

A fixed kiosk limits access and comfort for extended reading. The fix: a portable lap-desk device, checked out through the library's existing device-lending program rather than tied to one seat.

Public space integration

5–10 units dock and charge at the circulation desk, comparable to Carnegie Library's existing laptop lending cart — a 3–4 hour loan window, full recharge within 2 hours of return.

Reading Journey

Stage 1 Approaching the station Reader borrows PageLens and sits down; it wakes and auto-positions to their seat before they scan their library card and book.
Stage 2 Recognition & onboarding Returning readers jump straight into their saved settings; new readers pick an age band and choose which of the four features to turn on.
Stage 3 Reading session Selected features run live as the reader turns pages — a short tap simplifies a word, a long press reads a sentence aloud.
Stage 4 Leaving the station Removing the book ends the session; a brief reading summary appears, then the station clears all guest data and resets.

Across all failure states, PageLens follows one principle: degrade quietly, not loudly.

— Process book, Edge Cases & Error States

If the book isn't recognized, a manual title search keeps the session going with only Bring Story to Life disabled — everything else works as normal. If a page fails to process, a loading indicator holds the screen rather than showing an error. If a scan or age selection is wrong, there's no penalty; settings can be changed at any point. Nothing halts the reading session because the technology stumbled.

Ethics & Reflections

PageLens is built around the social model of disability: the barrier isn't the reader, it's the text.

— Process book, Accessibility & Ethical Considerations · April 2026

Two ethical questions shaped the project as much as any interaction decision. First, AI-generated illustration: the Bring Story to Life feature could lean entirely on generative images, but that raises a real concern for human illustrators. In the current concept, AI generation is treated as a last resort — the system sources licensed, existing illustrations first, labels any AI output clearly when it's necessary, and the team is committed to partnering with and fairly compensating human illustrators as the product develops.

Second, where the design still falls short: age-based personalization works for readers comfortable with a touchscreen, but users with limited digital literacy — older adults in particular — may struggle with the interface, and users with visual or motor impairments beyond the current feature set may find the experience insufficient. Naming that limit directly, rather than implying PageLens solves accessibility broadly, felt like part of the design work rather than a caveat tacked on at the end.
