KB Tutor

An adaptive biology tutoring platform for Title I high school students preparing for Pennsylvania's Keystone exam — built across five MVPs with real classroom data and a real teacher shaping every decision.

The Problem

Students were clicking through.
Not learning.

Pennsylvania's Keystone Biology exam has a persistently low passing rate, and in Title I schools that gap is even wider. Our teacher Abbe put it plainly: the district's existing diagnostic tool — the CDT — was useless. Students had learned they could click through without reading, because the feedback wasn't meaningful and it didn't count toward their grade. The data it produced told her nothing about what to re-teach.

The real problem wasn't content coverage. It was that students had never been taught to reason through biology — only to memorize it. And no tool on the market asked them to do anything different.

Root Cause

Students pattern-matched answers from memory rather than applying biological reasoning. Memorization was a rational response to a system that never rewarded anything else.

Teacher Gap

No tool surfaced which specific misconceptions were most common, or flagged which students needed intervention before the exam — only scores that didn't say what to do next.

Feedback Gap

Existing tools told students they were wrong. Not why, not what misconception drove it, not what to think instead.

Format Gap

The Keystone exam includes constructed-response questions requiring written causal explanations. No practice tool addressed this format at all.

Process

Five MVPs.
One through-line.

Every sprint started from the same question: which mechanism in our product is supposed to fix the learning problem, and how do we know if it's working? This kept us from building features that felt good but didn't trace back to anything a student actually needed.

01
Key Decision

Designing the
three-mode system

The core design question in MVP 1 was: how do you build a practice tool that forces conceptual reasoning instead of enabling memorization? The answer was modes students can move between freely based on what they need — not a fixed sequence. A student cramming before a test can jump straight to Exam; a teacher who sees a class struggling with a standard can assign that group into Practice for it directly.

Mode Job Scaffolding logic
Practice Surface the misconception. Students attempt first — hints and glossary only appear after a wrong answer, so they can't keyword-match their way through. Conditional on failure
Exam Test for genuine transfer. No scaffolding, no second chances. The gap between Practice and Exam accuracy is the most honest signal we have about whether learning actually happened. None

Review started as a third mode alongside these two, but as of MVP 5 it's no longer one of the practice modes at all — it lives as its own tab in the student dashboard, auto-populated with each student's missed questions and anything they've bookmarked.

The theory of change grounding this was simple: a wrong answer exposes a misconception → feedback targets that specific distractor → the corrected understanding gets tested in Exam mode → teacher uses the pattern data to decide what to re-teach. Every feature had to trace back to one of those four stages, regardless of which mode a student started in or how a teacher sequenced an assignment. If it didn't, it didn't ship.

MVP 3 validated the core loop — 76.3% of wrong-first items were eventually corrected in Practice, and students showed a statistically significant +9.1 percentage point accuracy gain moving from Practice to Exam. But it also surfaced a real problem: Review mode showed a median 5-second time-on-task per item across all attempts. Students weren't reading the feedback. They were clicking through — the exact habit we'd set out to break.

Showing a student the same explanation they already ignored doesn't create a new path to understanding.

— Insight from MVP 3 that shaped later prioritization

The 5-second finding reshaped how we thought about Review — but MVP 5 didn't end up fixing it by changing Review's feedback. Instead, two other things shipped: adaptivity was added to Practice mode, and Review was pulled into its own dashboard tab so it stopped competing with Practice and Exam for the same UI slot. Review's feedback content is still identical to Practice's — the differentiation this insight called for remains unbuilt.

02
Key Decision

Building the teacher
dashboard from Abbe's questions

The teacher dashboard started with one question Abbe asked us directly: "Can I see which standards I need to re-teach tomorrow?" Everything followed from that. My job was to translate what she actually needed into a feature list, then into a prioritized build sequence — separating what was essential on day one from what was good-to-have later.

Standards overview with status flags

Accuracy by standard across all three modes, with "Needs Review" and "On Track" labels so Abbe can scan the whole class in seconds rather than reading every number.

CDT flag: low accuracy + fast time

Surfaces students who are clicking through without reading. Time-on-task was the specific signal Abbe named — it's what the old diagnostic tool never surfaced and what made its data worthless.

Student-level progress view

Accuracy over time per student, filterable by assignment and standard. Abbe's exact words: a student with 11/12 correct looks identical to one with 99/100 in a snapshot view. She needed the trajectory, not the score.

Metric tooltips

On-hover definitions for every number. Abbe asked what "correct" meant — whether it was first-attempt or final-attempt accuracy. Now the answer is one hover away.

Priority misconception alerts

High confidence + wrong answer is the most dangerous pattern: the student actively believes something incorrect. More practice alone won't fix it. The dashboard flags these for individual teacher intervention.

Assignment scheduling

Teachers can sequence and release assignments on a schedule. Added after Abbe flagged that students working ahead of the class was disrupting her pacing — a constraint we hadn't anticipated.

One design distinction that shaped the whole dashboard: the difference between data that describes and data that directs. A percentage score describes. A "Needs Review" flag with a drill-down to the specific questions students missed — that directs. Every metric we included was evaluated against the question: can a teacher act on this, or does it just tell them something they already knew?

"I could identify the flags right away — this is exactly what I needed to know which concepts to re-teach."

— Abbe, classroom teacher · post-pilot feedback · May 2026

Reflections

What the five MVPs don't show.

The scaffolding worked. What it couldn't fix is a student who's spent years learning to click through things — unless the design earns their attention at every step, not just the first one.

— KB Tutor MVP 3 Evaluation  ·  BioBridge Team  ·  2026

This project ran far more condensed and technically involved than a five-MVP list can show. Underneath the interface decisions above sits an AI-generated question and feedback pipeline built to scale across both MCQ and SAQ formats, plus the adaptive routing logic that decides what a student sees next — each requiring real research, not just design polish. Anchoring every feature to the theory of change gave the team a shared language for saying no when scope crept; designing with Abbe from day one, not as a final check, meant the dashboard was usable before the pilot even started. My role ran the full stack of that process:

Low Fidelity

Figma

Started in Figma, exploring low-fi concepts for the diagnostic flow before committing to any single direction.

Interface Exploration

Vibe coding

As AI tooling matured, shifted from static mockups to vibe-coding — building working interface directly to explore ideas faster and finalize decisions Figma alone couldn't validate.

Consistency at Speed

A design system as claude.md

Wrote a claude.md design system so every vibe-coded screen stayed on-brand, and any further AI-assisted iteration extended the same visual language instead of drifting from it.

Data & Backend

Partnering with the developer

Worked with the developer on the data and backend — the BKT adaptivity model, the AI content pipeline — while owning the interface and product decisions end to end.

Validation

User testing loops

Ran repeated rounds of user testing with students and Abbe, and fed the feedback straight into design decisions — the CDT flag, the metric tooltips, and assignment scheduling all trace back to a specific testing round, not a hunch.

Self Reflection

Throughout this project I picked up a handful of things that go beyond any single MVP. Being the PM meant I got to see the product from a level up — not just designing a screen, but deciding which features were worth building at all, which was some of the most genuinely interesting work in the whole project. A big part of that was learning how to turn feedback, from Abbe and from round after round of user testing, into an actual design decision rather than just a note to revisit later.

None of it happened alone. Working with the developer on data and backend, staying in sync with the project manager, and pulling in what the researchers were seeing — collaborating across all of that was some of the most helpful and most challenging work of the project, and it shaped decisions I wouldn't have reached by myself.

As AI tooling matured over the course of the project, I also learned to use vibe coding deliberately rather than as a shortcut: a way to explore ideas quickly, polish the interface myself, and take some of the implementation pressure off the developer instead of just handing over a static mockup and hoping it translated.
