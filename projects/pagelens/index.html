PageLens is an AI-powered AR reading companion for physical library books. Grounded in accessibility research and technical feasibility, the system lets readers adapt spacing, simplify unfamiliar words, listen along, and reveal illustrations at meaningful story moments.

01 · Problem

Physical books are fixed.
Readers are not.

Reading needs vary across people and moments, yet the printed page offers one permanent presentation. The opportunity was to preserve the tactile, focused experience of a physical book while giving readers meaningful control.

01

Fixed

Printed typography, spacing, and page layout cannot adapt to the needs of an individual reader.

02

Disconnected

Reading support often moves people away from the physical book into a separate digital experience.

03

Visible

Specialized assistive tools can single readers out instead of supporting them with dignity.

How might we preserve the physical reading experience while giving every reader more control?

02 · Process

From library context
to a feasible system.

We moved between human evidence and technical detail: observing the service context, mapping the experience, evaluating technology, and testing whether the whole concept could behave responsibly.

We pressure-tested every feature against accessibility, dignity, data minimization, and reader control.

Decision 01

Design from evidence, not accessibility myths

UX Research · Typography · Evidence-led design

Context
Our early exploration included the common assumption that a specialized dyslexia font should be the foundation of the experience.
Problem
Research did not support treating one font as a universal solution. Readers benefit from different combinations of spacing, line length, contrast, and support.
Decision
I shifted the design system toward customizable character spacing, shorter line lengths, clear hierarchy, and optional visual support—giving readers control instead of prescribing one answer.
Design artifactReading settings that adapt to the individual
Decision 02

Offer capabilities, not a diagnosis

Design Ethics · Progressive Disclosure · Accessibility

Context
The concept needed to support people with varied reading needs without requiring them to disclose or accept a label.
Problem
A dedicated “Dyslexia Mode” would imply a single experience, expose sensitive information, and risk making the reader feel singled out.
Decision
I organized support around what readers want to do: Easy to Read, Simplify Words, Read Aloud, and Bring Story to Life. Each tool stays optional and can be combined on the reader’s terms.
Design artifactSupport described through actions, not labels

03 · Reflection

Accessible design supports
autonomy and dignity.

This project changed how I think about accessibility. The goal is not only to make content easier to read—it is to let people choose their support without being labeled, singled out, or asked to surrender privacy. Research moved us away from intuitive solutions and toward a flexible system readers could make their own.
