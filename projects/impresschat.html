01 · Problem

Online art learning is passive.
Looking is an active skill.

Articles, videos, and static explanations deliver information, but they rarely help learners practice looking closely, interpreting visual evidence, and connecting observation to context.

01

Passive

Learners consume information instead of practicing the visual reasoning that art understanding requires.

02

Content-heavy

Art history introduces unfamiliar terminology and historical context quickly, creating avoidable cognitive load.

03

Low interaction

Learners get few chances to explain what they see, test an interpretation, or receive immediate feedback.

02 · Process

From learning goals
to interaction design.

Backward design kept the interface grounded in learning outcomes. Each round of prototyping and testing made the content more conversational, accessible, and usable for first-time learners.

Stage 01

Backward design

Learning science · Outcomes

I began with the abilities learners should leave with: close observation, interpretation, and evidence-based reasoning.

Stage 02

Learning objectives

Curriculum · Scaffolding

Objectives became a sequence of foundation checks, terminology support, visual comparison, and application.

Stage 03

Dialogue first

Low-fi · Conversation flow

Wireframes focused on message length, learner response, and feedback cadence before visual polish.

Stage 04

Think aloud

Testing · Cognitive load

Observing first-time learners revealed where long messages and unfamiliar terms interrupted understanding.

Stage 05

Build & refine

Figma Make · User testing

I built the interactive prototype, tested the complete experience, and refined pacing, hierarchy, and support.

Key design decisions

Less content at once.
More learning in motion.

Decision 01

Design for conversation, not content delivery

Interaction Design · Cognitive Load · Iteration

Context
The chat interface needed to teach art concepts while still feeling conversational and lightweight.
Problem
Think-aloud testing showed that long instructional messages felt like a textbook placed inside chat bubbles.
Decision
I broke the content into shorter, sequential message chunks and adjusted the pacing between them. Learners could scan the conversation and keep response options in view.
Product screenFrom dense instruction to paced dialogue
Decision 02

Keep support available without interrupting the flow

Information Architecture · Progressive Disclosure · Accessibility

Context
Art history relies on terms such as brushstroke, composition, and perspective that many first-time learners do not yet know.
Problem
Explaining every term inline made the mobile experience dense, while removing definitions risked leaving learners behind.
Decision
I introduced essential vocabulary before the lesson, then highlighted unfamiliar terms in context with definitions available on demand.
Product screenSupport that appears at the moment of need

03 · Reflection

Design beyond
my own understanding.

Concepts that felt obvious to me were not obvious to first-time learners. User testing exposed that expert blind spot and helped me rethink content density, terminology, and pacing. Good design came from observing the experience learners actually had—not the one I expected.
